Learning Orbit — stripped and expanded

Learning Orbit — stripped

01 Encounter First exposure. Anchored to what the learner already knows. No testing — only contact. Plant the seed
02 Practice Immediate low-stakes application. Errors are signal, not score. Test the grip
03 Return The concept orbits back — timed by forgetting, not by calendar. Fight the fade
04 Apply Used inside a real problem. The concept must be recognised, not just recalled. Test the transfer
05 Build Woven into something larger. The learner feels how it connects to other blocks. Find the neighbours
06 Teach Explain it out loud. If you can't teach it, you don't own it. Prove the depth
07 Mastery Settled — but still orbiting. Nothing gets marked permanently complete. Widen the orbit

New constructs — built on the orbit

Every great learning tradition — Feynman, Socrates, Gurukul, Spaced Repetition — discovered the same thing. Knowledge only becomes yours when it survives contact with forgetting, contradiction, and teaching.

The constructs below are what that looks like inside TheDen.
Feynman Method
Feynman Loop
Richard Feynman — "If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it."
After every major concept, the engine asks: explain this to a 12-year-old. It evaluates clarity, not vocabulary. Jargon is penalised. Simple, accurate language is rewarded. If the explanation fails — the engine doesn't say "wrong." It says: let's go back one block.
Socratic Method
Socratic Pressure
Socrates — knowledge revealed through questions, not answers.
The engine never confirms an answer immediately. It asks: why do you think that? What breaks this? When does it fail? The learner has to defend their understanding before it gets stamped as known. Confidence earned through pressure lasts.
Gurukul / Vedic Tradition
Shruti Mode
Sanskrit — "that which is heard." Knowledge transmitted through repetition and rhythm.
Certain foundational concepts — syntax rules, algorithms, formulas — are returned repeatedly in micro-bursts across sessions. Not tested. Just heard again. Like a mantra, repetition builds unconscious fluency. The learner stops thinking about it and starts using it.
Spaced Repetition Science
Ebbinghaus Engine
Hermann Ebbinghaus — the forgetting curve, 1885.
The engine models each concept's decay rate individually. A concept mastered under stress decays faster than one earned through deep practice. Return intervals are not fixed — they're recalculated after every interaction. The orbit tightens when the learner struggles. It widens when they're solid.
Aristotelian Logic
Aristotle Chain
Aristotle — first principles reasoning, causes before effects.
Before a learner touches advanced concepts, the engine verifies the chain of reasoning beneath it. Not just dependencies — but the logical why. Why does this exist? What problem does it solve? Understanding the cause makes the concept unforgettable. Memorising the effect makes it fragile.
Indian Ascetic Tradition
Tapas Mode
Sanskrit — "heat through discipline." Mastery through deliberate struggle.
When a learner is ready, the engine removes scaffolding. No hints. No partial credit. A hard problem, clean slate, full pressure. Struggle is not a bug here — it's the feature. Research shows that retrieval under difficulty embeds knowledge deeper than any supported practice session.
Peer Learning Science
Echo Chamber
Vygotsky — the zone of proximal development. Learning accelerates between peers.
The learner teaches another learner — or a simulated peer. The engine plays the confused student: asking naïve questions, making plausible errors. The learner has to catch them, correct them, and explain. Teaching a peer reveals blind spots that no test ever finds.
Platonic Philosophy
Anamnesis
Plato — all learning is remembering. Knowledge is drawn out, not poured in.
The engine never tells the learner something they're close to figuring out. It asks a sequence of questions that leads them to the answer themselves. The moment of self-discovery is encoded differently in memory than received information. What you discover, you own.
